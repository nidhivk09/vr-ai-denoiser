How it works · two eyes

Why each eye sees a different image

Seen from above. The two eyes look in the same direction, 63 mm apart. Drag the sphere. When it comes closer, it lands in very different places in the two images. Far away, the two images almost agree. Your brain turns that difference, the disparity, into depth. This is why the renderer traces both eyes, and why the denoiser must keep them consistent.

fpx = 703 px is the focal length of a 512-pixel-wide image with a 40° field of view, as in our renders. The eye separation is drawn 3× wider than real so the lines are easy to see. The numbers use the real 63 mm.

"Overlay both eyes" draws the left image in red and the right in cyan on top of each other. That is exactly how the anaglyph view at the top of the page works.

Monte Carlo noise

More samples, less noise, more time

Each pixel is the average of random light paths. The error falls as 1/√N, so halving the noise costs four times the samples. A headset needs a new frame every 11.1 ms (90 FPS) for each eye, which leaves room for only a few samples. That gap is what the denoiser fills.

Inside the renderer

More than a colour image

Each frame also writes noise-free buffers from the first surface hit. They tell the denoiser where edges and textures are. The work map counts the BVH nodes and triangles each pixel tests. Bright pixels are expensive, and when they sit next to cheap ones the GPU threads diverge.

Next-event estimation

The light is small, so random bounces rarely hit it. NEE fixes this by sending a shadow ray straight to the light at every bounce. Drag the slider: both halves use 16 samples per pixel.

The integrator is a single header shared by the multithreaded CPU backend and the CUDA kernels (__host__ __device__), so both devices run the same algorithm.

Stereo geometry

Depth from two viewpoints

d = IPD · fpx / Z Disparity d in pixels for a point at depth Z, with IPD = 0.063 (63 mm) and fpx the focal length in pixels. The same point appears at xright = xleft − d. Unit tests check this convention in both the C++ renderer and the Python training code, because a sign error here would still train without any visible error.
Neural denoiser

A few samples in, a clean pair out

A U-Net takes the noisy colour plus the normal and albedo buffers and predicts the clean image. It was trained on procedurally generated scenes and is shown here on a scene it never saw. The stereo version adds a loss that warps the right eye's output into the left eye and penalises any difference.

Where the two eyes disagree

|left − warp(right)|, ×5, on pixels visible to both eyes. In a headset, disagreement shows up as shimmer.
